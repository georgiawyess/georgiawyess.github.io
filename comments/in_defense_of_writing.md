<h1>In Defense of Writing</h1>
<p><em>November 2026</em></p>

<p>The <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/09/29/opinion/writing-thinking-college-ai.html" target="_blank">NYTimes article “I'm a College Professor. Writing isn’t as important as we think.”</a> by guest contributor Joe Cruz left me with a number of thoughts. What I want to focus on here is the specific interplay between writing and thinking. I understand Cruz’ argument to say that writing itself isn’t central to thinking and I have to agree to some extent. You do need to have a semi-formulated thought before you can write an intelligible piece of writing. However, you can’t ignore that writing forces you to interact with your ideas on an externalized medium. When you think, your ideas remain your own, and you are the only one engaging with them until you share them meaning you’re likely to drive ideas to conform with your own innate biases and make logical jumps that make sense to you. Externalizing an argument makes it such that thoughts need to be intelligibly formulated to be shared. This allows you to argue with yourself to reinforce your ideas more intentionally. 
</p>

<p>The natural question then is does speaking allow for development of ideas the same way that writing does? It’s a means to externalize ideas and have others engage in them. Here, I think writing is still the stronger of the two. In speech you have the opportunity to explain your ideas in long-winded ways and for those engaging in conversation with you, there is a pressure to respond in the moment. Fully formed opinions and ideas on what you have shared may not fully develop until later — in my own experience, I always think of my best responses two or three hours after the conversation has ended, often in the car ride home. When you engage in dialogue to share your ideas, they’re not as deeply engaged with, and others will sometimes simply agree rather than debate. In writing, people have the opportunity to ruminate on your words and engage with them multiple lines by reading and rereading sections of your statements. 
</p>

<p>Cruz claims that you can conduct an entire lecture as oral based discussion, or test students through oral exams, but the truth is they don’t require as much thought. Instead, writing and thinking should be approached as stages in the same sequence. Oral expiration of an idea is a fantastic means of familiarizing yourself with where you stand on a given subject, or where your knowledge gaps are. Writing can as well. And it’s true that your brain can more freely wander when you’re speaking which may lead to new ideas but in this is where the problem lies: it’s  hard to focus in on one idea. Writing, here, does something that a conversation can’t reproduce as well.  
</p>

<p>Writing isn’t necessarily what leads to ideas, but it does allow you to more thoughtfully engage with what you’re saying. Bad ideas are particularly hard to write down. They require logical conclusions that would be hard to concisely explain on paper, and the end result wouldn’t make much sense either. The act of writing down your ideas tests the ability to connect your thoughts and form a logical web that is intelligible to others. If your idea itself is bad, then the leads required of one to make sense of what you’re saying will likely cause your idea to fail.
</p>

<p>So here is an assumption that I think Cruz makes that doesn’t quite hold true: People engage with ideas as meaningfully as they do in their heads, as they do on paper. I wish this were true. I wish I could simply keep thinking about something with zero distractions until I can produce a magnum opus in one go. The fact of the matter is, this isn’t how thinking works. Especially in schools. Student’s don’t have an understanding of a certain subject matter prior to coming into lecture. They need to write down ideas and engage with them to build a foundation. Cruz can afford to think aloud because he’s already done all the thinking ahead of time; he’s a professor that knows what he’s talking about.
</p>

<p>I think this also matters beyond academic environments. A rise in social media has led to an environment where publishing ideas without fact-checking is acceptable, or engaging with long-form content is no longer a norm. Scrolling is easy. Emotionally charged, unbacked arguments that exist to mobilize the masses rather than educate them win clicks. Writing posts on X that eludes all context can be done in seconds. Writing something that stands the test of time is hard. As such, the less practice people get in producing good ideas, the less they’re able to spot them or analyze the ideas of others. If college is the last place people get such experience, then telling students that writing isn’t important doesn’t signal its value.
</p>

<p>Cruz has a point that talking is a good way to <em>start</em> thinking. But if this is where all thought were tested, then we would never know which ideas would hold, and which would fail. I think the ability to write generally allows us to produce good ideas, and the ability to write well comes from engaging with well-written arguments. The relationship is cyclical. The less we write, the less we are able to recognize when we are interacting with ideas that are maybe not worth our time.
</p>
